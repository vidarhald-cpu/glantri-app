## Care of people and living things

### K14 — Bedside attendance practice

**Short description.** Sustained attendance, observation, reporting and the
household conduct it requires.

An attendant keeps company with a sick or injured person hour after hour, and
learns the work by doing it beside someone who has done it longer. The
correction is constant and specific: you turned the patient too late, you
missed that the breathing changed, you should have called someone an hour ago.
What is being trained is not a body of theory but a quality of attention
sustained over a long watch, and the judgment of when that watch has turned
into something that needs a physician.

Attendance may be taught in a household as readily as in an organized place of
care, and where it is, the household is part of the curriculum. An attendant
works inside someone else's home, among their family, their servants and their
griefs, and is expected to know how to conduct themselves there. A learner who
nurses competently but blunders through the household does not last.

**What it teaches.** Sustained practical care and observation of someone ill or
recovering; the immediate assessment and first response when their condition
turns; and the social conventions of the households and communities the
attendant works within.

**Boundaries and context.** This is the attendant's practice, not the
physician's. It does not teach the assessment of illness and the direction of
treatment — an attendant who has learned here reports a change and calls for
someone who can judge it. Attendance and medicine are taught together
elsewhere; here they are deliberately apart.

### K15 — Birth-attendance practice

**Short description.** Specialist birth care taught together with the medical
grounding it requires.

Birth attendance is learned at births, from an experienced attendant, over
enough of them that the ordinary course becomes familiar and the departures
from it become recognizable. The learner assists, then assists less, then
manages a straightforward birth while the teacher watches. Afterwards the two
of them go back over what was done and why — which signs mattered, which were
noise, and at what point a difficult birth stopped being one the attendant
could manage alone.

The practice does not teach the specialist care in isolation. A birth attendant
who cannot assess a patient generally, care for her through a recovery, or deal
with an immediate emergency is not a birth attendant. The general medical
grounding is taught alongside the specialism because the specialism does not
work without it.

**What it teaches.** The specialist care of pregnancy, childbirth and maternal
recovery, within the tradition's own understanding of them; the assessment of
illness and injury and the direction of treatment; sustained practical care of
a recovering patient; and immediate response when something goes wrong.

**Boundaries and context.** The tradition taught is a particular one, with its
own understanding of the expected course of a birth and its own methods.
Learning it does not confer surgical practice, and part of what is trained is
recognizing the case that exceeds what the attendant's methods can do.

### K13 — Stock medicine practice

**Short description.** Differentiated animal clinical instruction supported by
recurring cases.

Animal medicine is learned where there are enough animals for cases to recur.
The learner works through a season or several with someone who treats stock:
the same complaints come back, in different animals and different conditions,
and the teacher's correction turns a first guess into a differentiated
judgment. What distinguishes this from general familiarity with animals is that
the learner is held to a clinical standard — what is actually wrong with this
animal, as against what is usually wrong with animals that look like this.

The practice is inseparable from the keeping of the animals. A treatment
prescribed without regard to how the beast is fed, housed and worked will not
be carried out, and the practitioner who cannot handle a frightened animal
cannot examine it. Husbandry and handling are taught here not as background but
because the clinical work is impossible without them.

**What it teaches.** The assessment and treatment of illness and injury in
animals; the sustained management of domestic animals through feeding, housing,
breeding and routine care; and the practical handling that lets an animal be
directed and worked with.

**Boundaries and context.** This is instruction in treating animals, not
people. It is a practice of the working stock keeper and the animal doctor;
knowledge of wild animals and their ways is a different competence, learned
elsewhere.

### K12 — Naturalist field study

**Short description.** Comparing living things in the field and recording what
the comparison shows.

The naturalist's practice is deliberate comparison. The learner is sent out to
find, observe and describe living things, and then brought back to account for
the description: what exactly was seen, how it differed from the last one, what
was assumed rather than observed. Over time the corrections shift from
identification to method — the learner stops collecting impressions and starts
collecting evidence that will still mean something a year later.

It is a study practice conducted outdoors rather than a field craft conducted
studiously. The learner is taught to hold a question, gather what bears on it,
weigh it and say honestly what it supports. That the material is animals and
plants is what gives the practice its subject; that it is pursued as
investigation is what makes it this Group rather than the practical woodcraft
of someone who hunts.

**What it teaches.** The recognition of animals and understanding of their
behaviour, habitats and ways of living; the identification of plants and
knowledge of their habitats, characteristics and useful properties; and the
deliberate gathering, comparison and evaluation of evidence to answer a
question.

**Boundaries and context.** Naturalist study is not a practical outdoor
survival training, nor a remedy practice: knowing a plant well is not the same
as preparing it. It teaches how living things are known and recorded, not what
is done with them.

## Water

### K20 — Diving and underwater work practice

**Short description.** Working below the surface on a line, and seeing what is
down there.

Diving for work is taught by divers, in the water, on a short line and then a
longer one. The learner descends, does something deliberate below the surface
and comes up, and is corrected on all of it: the breath, the descent, the
handling of the line, and above all on the discipline of coming up before
having to. It is repetitive, deliberately unexciting training, because the
practice being built is the ability to work calmly at depth rather than to
reach it.

Most of what a diver does below the surface is done with a rope. A line
commonly serves as signal, lifeline and working tackle at once — objects may be
secured and sent up on it, and it is the diver's link to the surface. Handling
rope well is not an adjacent convenience here; it is half the work, and it is
taught to a working standard.
The other half is seeing: water gives poor light, deceptive distance and little
time, and a diver is trained to find and recognize a thing under those
conditions rather than to look around.

**What it teaches.** Trained movement and support of the body in water; the
skilled tying, joining, securing and practical use of ropes and lines; and
attentive noticing and discrimination of relevant detail, including during a
deliberate search.

**Boundaries and context.** This is working diving — salvage, clearing,
recovery, inspection — taught by people who do it for a living. It is not the
handling of a vessel, and it is not a swimming pastime; a person who swims well
has not been through this.

### N01 — Small-craft boat working

**Short description.** Working an open boat: handling, lines, water and
weather.

Working an open boat is a whole practice, and it is learned as one. The learner
goes out in the boat with someone who has worked it for years, takes an oar and
then the tiller, makes fast and casts off, and is corrected continuously and
often sharply, because in a small boat a mistake is immediate. There is no
classroom stage: the instruction is the work, repeated across enough days and
conditions that handling stops being deliberate.

The practice is larger than steering. Everything in an open boat is held by
rope, and the learner's knots are inspected until they are right. Everyone who
works one is expected to be able to swim, and swimming is taught within the
practice rather than assumed. And the decision that matters most is made
ashore: a boat's crew learns to read the sky, the water and the season, and to
judge
whether to go out at all. These are taught together because the boat does not
separate them.

**What it teaches.** The practical handling of a vessel and the working
routines needed to operate it on water; the skilled use of ropes and lines;
trained movement and support of the body in water; and the interpretation of
environmental signs and recurring conditions to judge likely weather.

**Boundaries and context.** This is the open boat — the ferry, the working
skiff, the harbour and inshore craft. It is not the rigged deep-water vessel
with its masts and aloft work, and it is not chart and instrument navigation.
It teaches a boat and the water it works in, not a passage across an ocean.

## Arms, drill and service

### M01 — Close-combat weapon practice

**Short description.** Guard, defence and one actually taught weapon family.

A weapon course teaches a weapon. The learner is put opposite a teacher or a
fellow learner with the arms of one family, and worked through guard, approach,
strike and recovery until the movements are no longer decided one at a time.
The corrections are on the whole of it — the grip, the footing, the distance,
the habit of leaving oneself open after a blow — and they continue until the
learner's defence holds up against someone genuinely trying to get past it.

Which family is taught depends entirely on the teacher and the tradition they
come out of. A course is a course in the sword, or in the axe and mace, or in
the spear and staff, and a learner comes out of it trained in that family
rather than in weapons generally. Defence is not treated as a separate subject:
getting clear of an attack and meeting one with the weapon are taught as part of
learning to fight with it, because they cannot usefully be separated.

**What it teaches.** Trained bodily movement that avoids a direct attack;
trained interception and redirection of an attack through the established
parrying practice; and one melee weapon family — the one the course actually
teaches, whether that is the one-handed edged weapons, the one-handed club,
mace, pick and axe, the two-handed edged weapons, the two-handed concussion and
axe weapons, or the staff, spear and pole weapons.

**Boundaries and context.** One family, not a survey of arms. Unarmed fighting
is a different practice and is not taught here: a weapon course teaches the
weapon. Neither is this the experience of actual fighting — a learner who has
completed it has been trained, not blooded.

### M02 — Advanced close-combat weapon practice

**Short description.** Sustained instruction across several fighting forms at
close quarters.

Some teachers, and some schools, keep a learner for years rather than a season,
and what they produce is different in kind. The learner is taken across several
forms of close fighting in turn and then against each other — the sword against
the spear, the thrown weapon against the closing man, the grapple when the
weapon is gone — until what is trained is not a weapon but fighting at close
quarters with whatever the moment supplies. The corrections move from technique
to judgment: not how the cut is made, but why this fight was answered that way.

Such a school's range is genuinely broad, and deliberately so. A master of close
combat is expected to have been taught the grapple and the thrown weapon
alongside the blade, because at the distances this practice concerns itself with
they are the same problem. The learner does not take all of it; a course of
instruction covers what its master actually teaches, which is several forms and
not every form.

**What it teaches.** Trained evasion and trained parrying, as the constant
ground of everything else; and several fighting forms at close quarters, drawn
from the melee weapon families, trained unarmed fighting at close range, and the
delivery of a hand-thrown weapon.

**Boundaries and context.** This is instruction, however long and however
thorough, and instruction cannot supply what only being in a real fight
supplies. A master trained here may never have stood in a battle line. The
overlap with the basic and formation courses is real and intended: a fighter who
has been through more than one has been taught more than once.

### M03 — Formation arms drill

**Short description.** Close-order drill with the arms the formation actually
carries.

Drill in a formed body is a different training from individual weapon
instruction, and it is not gentler. Learners are put in the ranks and worked
until the body moves as one: closing up, holding the line, advancing and
halting together, keeping the weapon where the man beside them expects it to be.
The correction is collective and immediate, and much of what is being trained
is the suppression of individual improvisation that would open a gap.

A unit is drilled in the arms it is issued, and units are issued more than one.
Which arms those are depends on the unit: two families of line weapon in one,
or a line weapon together with whatever the unit throws or slings at an enemy
it has not yet closed with in another, where the same men are expected to
change between them on command. Guard and defence are drilled too, in the
particular narrow form that the ranks allow — there is little room to step aside
in close order, and learning what one can and cannot do there is part of the
drill.

**What it teaches.** The practiced conduct of an individual fighting as part of
an organized formation; trained evasion and trained parrying as the formation
permits them; and the arms the formation actually carries and drills, drawn from
the melee weapon families, the hand-thrown weapon and the sling.

**Boundaries and context.** Close-order drill is not an unarmed course, and
nothing here teaches wrestling or the fist. Nor is it individual mastery: a
drilled soldier is competent in a formation and may be much less so alone.
Command of the formation is a separate practice from serving in it.

### M11 — Mounted combat practice

**Short description.** Fighting from the saddle, and the horsemanship that makes
it possible.

Fighting on horseback cannot be taught to someone who is still thinking about
the horse. The two are therefore taught as one course: the learner is put up,
ridden hard enough that control becomes habitual, and then required to do
something else while riding — reach, strike, turn, recover — with the teacher
correcting seat and hand together. What is corrected is the rider's part of
it — how the mount is asked, and what it can reasonably be asked for — rather
than the schooling of the horse, which belongs to a different practice.

What the practice trains is the coordination itself. A competent fighter on foot
and a competent rider are not, between them, a mounted fighter; the timing of a
blow delivered from a moving animal, the management of reins and weapon at once,
and the judgment of what the horse can be asked to do are learned only in the
saddle and only together.

**What it teaches.** The practiced control of and travel on a mount, and the
trained coordination of fighting with the movement and demands of that mount.

**Boundaries and context.** This is the individual horseman. It is not the drill
of a formed body of horse, and it does not teach the lance, which is its own
weapon with its own instruction.

### M12 — Mounted lance and formation drill

**Short description.** The lance, delivered in a formed body of horse.

The lance is drilled as a formation weapon. A line of riders arriving together
is the point of the weapon, and the training reflects that. Learners are
drilled at the lance and drilled in the
formed body at the same time, riding knee to knee at increasing speed, taking a
mark, and above all holding the line — because a charge that arrives ragged has
failed whatever each individual rider did.

The instruction is accordingly as much about the body as the weapon. Keeping
place and interval at speed, going in together and coming out together, and
re-forming afterwards are drilled until they survive noise and disorder. A rider
taught here has learned to deliver the lance as part of something, which is the
only way it is delivered.

**What it teaches.** The trained use of the lance weapon family, and the
practiced conduct of an individual fighting as part of an organized formation.

**Boundaries and context.** This is the lance and the charge, not mounted
fighting generally: the handling of the horse and of other weapons from the
saddle belongs to a different practice. Nor does it teach the command of a body
of horse, which is a separate thing from riding in one.

### M04 — Military missile and formation practice

**Short description.** Shooting in formation with the missile arm the unit is
issued.

Military shooting is usually drilled rather than individually coached. Learners
are formed up and shoot on command, together, at ranges and marks set for them,
and are corrected as much
on timing and obedience as on aim — a volley loosed raggedly is a failure even
if every shot was good. The drill continues until loading, presenting and
loosing are done without thought, because they will have to be done while
something is happening.

Two things are drilled alongside the shooting. The first is the weapon's
service: a missile arm that has not been looked after fails at the worst moment,
and every soldier issued one is taught to inspect, care for and keep it working.
The second is looking. A shooting line has to find its mark, judge its distance
and notice what is coming toward it, and that attentiveness is trained as
deliberately as the volley.

**What it teaches.** The practiced conduct of an individual fighting as part of
an organized formation; the inspection, care and working service of weapons; the
attentive noticing and discrimination of relevant detail; and one missile arm —
whichever the unit is issued and drilled with, whether the bow, the longbow,
the crossbow, handheld firearms, the sling or the hand-thrown weapon.

**Boundaries and context.** One arm, the one the unit carries. This is formation
shooting, so it does not teach close fighting, and it does not supply the
experience of real action.

### M05 — Advanced missile practice

**Short description.** Sustained instruction across several missile families.

A shooting master's course is long and narrow: several kinds of missile weapon,
taught properly and in turn. The learner is taken past marksmanship into the
differences between the arms — what each one does at
what distance, how each behaves in wind and wet, what each demands of the hand
that holds it — and is corrected against a standard well above a soldier's.
Range work is repeated past the point of competence, because the practice being
built is consistency rather than ability.

Keeping the weapons is inseparable from the instruction. A master shot who
cannot diagnose a failing bow or a fouled lock is not one, and the learner works
on the arms as well as with them. Attentiveness is trained to the same standard:
reading distance, light, air and target is where a master's advantage actually
lies.

**What it teaches.** Attentive noticing and discrimination of relevant detail;
the inspection, care and working service of weapons; and several missile arms
taught in turn, drawn from the bow, the longbow, the crossbow, handheld
firearms, the sling and the hand-thrown weapon.

**Boundaries and context.** It remains instruction. A shooting master who has
never been shot at has learned nothing here about being shot at, and the
practice does not pretend otherwise. It is also not formation drill: this is the
individual's shooting, taken a long way.

### M06 — Campaign service experience

**Short description.** What repeated service in the field teaches that drill
does not.

This is not a course, and there is no teacher. What is described here is
repeated service on campaign — seasons in the field, marches, camps, actions
and the long stretches between them — and what such service can teach someone
who does it. The correction is supplied by consequence rather than by
instruction. Presence with a campaign is not itself the practice: what counts is
repeated service of the qualifying kind, and someone whose duties keep them away
from it may come out of a campaign having learned much less.

Where the service is of that kind, three things are learned together. Fighting
stops being disorienting: noise,
confusion and fear remain, but they no longer prevent the person from doing
their work. A sense of the wider fight develops — where the danger is, what is
about to happen on the flank, when a position has become untenable — which no
drill ground produces because no drill ground contains it. And living rough
becomes ordinary: campaign is mostly weather, ground, hunger and shelter, and
someone who has done it knows how to manage them.

**What it teaches.** The practiced ability to function amid the pressure,
confusion and disruption of actual fighting; the practiced understanding of
threats and developments across an unfolding fight; and the practical management
of exposure and immediate needs away from dependable shelter and supplies.

**Boundaries and context.** This is exposure, not instruction, and it is the
central distinction the practice rests on. It teaches no weapon: a veteran has
learned to function in a fight, not to fence. Equally, no amount of teaching
substitutes for it.

### M10 — Arms instruction practice

**Short description.** Teaching others to fight, and keeping the weapons the
school uses.

Being able to fight and being able to teach fighting are different competences,
and the second is learned deliberately. A learner takes classes under the eye of
an established master, is watched while doing it, and is corrected on the
teaching: the explanation that confused rather than clarified, the demonstration
made at the wrong speed, the fault left uncorrected because it was awkward to
raise, the pace that lost half the class. What is being built is the ability to
get a competence into someone else.

A school's weapons are its stock in trade, and the instructor keeps them. Blunts
and practice arms are used hard by people who do not yet handle them well, and
somebody has to inspect, service and retire them. Learning this is part of
learning the trade, and it is a working standard rather than a craftsman's:
keeping a weapon serviceable, not making one.

**What it teaches.** The practice of helping another person learn through
explanation, demonstration, guided practice and useful correction; and the
inspection, care and working service of weapons.

**Boundaries and context.** Instructing is not exposure, and nothing here
supplies the experience of real fighting. Nor is this a conditioning course or a
weapon course in itself — it is the practice of teaching, and fighting itself is
learned elsewhere.

### M07 — Campaign staff practice

**Short description.** Planning, supplying and recording a campaign rather than
fighting it.

Staff work is learned in a headquarters, on an actual campaign, by doing small
parts of it under someone who does the large parts. The learner drafts, reckons,
lists and writes, and has the work sent back: the march table that did not
account for the river crossing, the supply estimate that assumed a road, the
order that could be read two ways. The corrections are unglamorous and
cumulative, and they are the practice.

The four things that make up staff work are learned together because in a
headquarters they are one problem. What the campaign is trying to achieve, what
it will take to feed and move, what the army should do when it makes contact,
and how all of it is recorded and passed on constrain one another too closely to
be settled apart. A plan that ignores supply is not a plan, and a decision that
is
not properly recorded and transmitted did not happen.

**What it teaches.** The planning and coordination of military operations toward
a wider campaign objective; the planning and maintenance of the supplies,
transport and replenishment a force needs; the judgment of what an organized
unit should do during an engagement; and the organization of recurring work,
resources, records and procedures so responsibilities are carried out
dependably.

**Boundaries and context.** This is the staff, not the line. It teaches no
weapon, no drill and no command of men in a fight; a capable staff officer may
be a poor soldier. It plans and sustains the campaign.

### M08 — Siege works practice

**Short description.** Designing and building the works a siege needs.

Siege works are learned on sieges and on the fortifications that provoke them.
The learner works under an engineer: examining the ground, laying out an
approach, designing a battery or a counterwork, and then watching it be built
and fail or hold. The correction is emphatic, because the ground supplies it —
a trench dug where it can be enfiladed, a platform that will not bear its
engine, a gallery that floods. Over several works the learner stops copying
forms and starts reading situations.

The engineer's competence is architectural and mechanical before it is military.
Everything in a siege is a structure that must stand under load — the battery
under its engine, the gallery under its ground, the bridge under a column — and
everything is a mechanism or moves one. A siege engineer is therefore taught as
a builder and a mechanic who applies both to attack and defence, and the same
training carries the specialist understanding of how a place is designed to be
defended: barriers, approaches, openings and positions related to the ground
around them.

**What it teaches.** The design and construction of works and mechanisms used in
attacking or supporting the defence of fortified places; the design of buildings
that meet their uses and remain structurally workable; the practical analysis of
forces, loads and motion in structures and machines; and the specialist design
of buildings and sites for defence.

**Boundaries and context.** This is the design and construction of works, not
the serving of engines, which is a separate practice with its own crew and its
own drill. It is not a command practice, it teaches no weapon, and the
systematic measurement of ground is a separate competence taught elsewhere.

### M09 — Siege engine crew practice

**Short description.** Serving, laying and keeping a siege engine with its crew.

An engine is served by a crew, and the crew is trained as a unit. Each member
learns their station and every station around it, and the whole is worked
through the cycle — loading, laying, discharging, resetting — until the sequence
runs at speed without anyone directing it. The corrections are to the crew as
much as to the individual, because an engine mishandled by one man endangers all
of them, and the drill continues until the dangerous mistakes are gone.

Two other things are drilled with it. An engine is a machine under great load,
and the crew keeps it: cordage, timbers, mechanism and missiles are inspected,
serviced and replaced by the men who work them, because nobody else is there to
do it. And the crew serves in a formed body under fire, often tied to the
engine's position, which is its own discipline and is drilled as one.

**What it teaches.** The placement, coordinated working and routine operational
management of siege engines; the practical analysis of forces, loads and motion
in machines; the practiced conduct of an individual fighting as part of an
organized formation; and the inspection, care and working service of weapons.

**Boundaries and context.** The crew serves the engine; it does not design it,
nor the works it stands in. This is not a close-combat or missile course: a
crewman is trained to work his engine in a formed position, not to fight as an
individual.

### K23 — Coercive service practice

**Short description.** Applying pressure on behalf of someone who pays for it.

This is not a course and nobody would call it one, but it is a practice, it is
sustained, and people are unmistakably corrected in it. Someone who collects
debts, enforces agreements or removes obstacles for an employer learns the work
by doing it repeatedly alongside others who do it, and the correction comes from
the employer, from the people leaned on, and from what happens when it is done
badly. What is learned is largely judgment: how far to go, how far is too far,
and which of the two mistakes is more expensive.

Real violence is part of it, and that is what distinguishes the practice from
any kind of instruction. A person in this line has been in actual fights, not
staged ones, often enough that fighting no longer disorganizes them. They have
also learned the milieu they operate in — who is owed what by whom, whose
protection is real, which quarrels are safe to take on — because operating
inside it without that knowledge does not last long.

**What it teaches.** The use of credible threat and forceful presentation to
pressure another person's decision; the practiced ability to function amid the
pressure, confusion and disruption of actual fighting; and practical knowledge
of the networks, conventions and dealings of a particular illicit milieu.

**Boundaries and context.** Exposure, not instruction: this practice teaches no
weapon and no drill. It is coercion in someone's service, which is what makes it
a sustained practice rather than an occasional act of violence.

## Workshops and extraction

### C01 — General artisan apprenticeship

**Short description.** An ordinary workshop apprenticeship in one actually
taught craft.

This is the ordinary shape of a working apprenticeship. A learner is placed with
a master for years, sweeps the shop, prepares materials, is given the simplest
real work and then progressively harder work, and has every piece of it looked
over and sent back until it passes. The craft learned is whichever craft the shop
practises — a potter's apprentice learns pottery, a smith's learns smithing —
and the apprentice comes out competent in that one and not in the others.

A workshop teaches more than the craft, and it teaches it as deliberately.
Material has to be bought and work has to be sold, and an apprentice is sent to
do both under supervision long before being trusted with the bench alone. Judging
whether a hide, a fleece, a billet or a finished piece is good, and settling a
price for it with someone who is also trying to settle one, are corrected as
sharply as bad workmanship, because a craftsman who cannot do them does not stay
in business.

**What it teaches.** The assessment of goods by their quality, condition,
workmanship and likely value; the negotiation of terms through proposals,
concessions and judgments about what the parties will accept; and one craft —
whichever the shop actually practises, among the pottery, weaving, fibre, leather,
timber, garment, brewing, stone, metal, cordage and sailmaking trades.

**Boundaries and context.** One craft, the shop's own. The trade sense taught
alongside it is the workshop's, not a merchant's: buying stock and selling work,
not conducting a trading house.

### C02 — Master artisan practice

**Short description.** Guild- or master-level practice in one taught craft,
with apprentices of one's own.

Passing out of an apprenticeship is not the end of learning a craft. A craftsman
who goes on to master or guild standing continues under correction, now from
peers and elders rather than a single master, and the standard applied is
different: not whether the work is acceptable but whether it is good enough to be
answerable for. What is being formed is a practitioner who can be trusted with a
shop, a name and other people's work.

The decisive addition is apprentices. A master has learners of his own and is
held responsible for what they become, and teaching at that level is learned —
badly at first, and corrected by the guild and by the results. The trade side
deepens accordingly, because a master judges and prices not only his own work but
his shop's, his apprentices', and often other people's brought to him for an
opinion.

**What it teaches.** The assessment of goods by their quality, condition,
workmanship and likely value; the negotiation of terms through proposals,
concessions and judgments about what the parties will accept; the practice of
helping another person learn through explanation, demonstration, guided practice
and useful correction; and one craft, at the standard the master's own shop
practises it.

**Boundaries and context.** Master standing is standing in a craft, not in
crafts: it does not make a master potter a mason. The teaching learned here is
the teaching of a trade to apprentices in a workshop.

### C04 — Leather and hide workshop

**Short description.** Turning hide into usable material and finished goods.

A leather shop takes in raw hide and sends out goods, and an apprentice is
walked through the whole of that passage. The early work is the unpleasant work —
the preparation, by whatever stages the shop's methods use, that turns skin into
leather — and it is where much of the correction falls, because a hide spoiled
at that stage
cannot be rescued later. Only afterwards does the learner get to cut, shape, join
and finish, and there the corrections become exacting about fit and wear.

The shop's other materials are learned with the leather. Almost nothing made of
leather is made only of leather: it is sewn, laced, lined and bound, and an
apprentice prepares and works the fibres and threads that do it. Judging hide is
the other constant lesson, and the hardest. Grain, thickness, damage and
condition decide what a skin can become and what it is worth, and an apprentice
is made to say which before being told.

**What it teaches.** The cutting, shaping, joining and finishing of leather into
serviceable goods; the preparation of raw fibres and their conversion into usable
thread and related materials; and the assessment of goods by their quality,
condition, workmanship and likely value.

**Boundaries and context.** A leather shop, not a garment shop: cutting and
fitting cloth into clothing is a different trade with a different training. Nor
does working hide teach the keeping of the animals it came from.

### C05 — Garment workshop practice

**Short description.** Cutting and making up cloth into fitted garments.

A garment shop's apprentice is taught to fit. The work begins with seams and
simple making-up, moves to cutting under close supervision, and is corrected
against bodies rather than patterns — a garment that measures correctly and hangs
wrong has failed, and the apprentice is made to see why. Cloth is expensive and a
bad cut wastes it, so the correction here is immediate and remembered.

Some shops of this kind make their own cloth, or enough of it that the trade is
taught alongside the cutting, and a tailor who does not understand weaving is
poorly placed to judge what he is cutting. Apprentices in such a shop work the
loom as well as the shears, and learn how a fabric is constructed, how it will
behave when cut across or
along, and how it will wear. Judging cloth and finished garments is the third
constant: what this bolt is, what it is worth, and whether the work in front of
them is good.

**What it teaches.** The cutting, fitting and joining of cloth into garments and
other fitted textile goods; the production of fabric through the controlled
interlacing of prepared threads or fibres; and the assessment of goods by their
quality, condition, workmanship and likely value.

**Boundaries and context.** Cloth and garments. Leatherwork is a separate trade,
and the heavy canvas of a sail loft, though also cut and sewn, is another
practice again.

### C09 — Sail and canvas workshop

**Short description.** Cutting, seaming and roping heavy canvas.

A sail loft is a floor, and the work is done on it. Apprentices learn by seaming
first, hour after hour, then by helping lay out and cut, and eventually by
working a sail through from cloth to finished article under the sailmaker's eye.
The corrections are about shape and strength: a sail has to set as its vessel and
rig require, and work that is merely neat has not met that test. What passes is
judged by how the sail sets and how long it lasts, not by its appearance in the
loft.

Canvas is a heavy fabric and the loft prepares and handles its own — an
apprentice learns the fibre, the thread and the twine as part of learning the
trade, because the seam is only as good as what it is sewn with. Rope is the
other half. Every sail is bounded, reinforced and attached by cordage, and
roping a sail is skilled rope work done to a standard the sea will test. The
loft therefore teaches lines and their working use as thoroughly as it teaches
the cloth.

**What it teaches.** The design, cutting, assembly and repair of sails suited to
a vessel and its rig; the preparation of raw fibres and their conversion into
usable thread and related materials; and the skilled tying, joining, securing and
practical use of ropes and lines.

**Boundaries and context.** This is the loft ashore. It makes and repairs the
sail and does not teach the working of the vessel that carries it. Fitted
clothing is a different trade.

### C06 — Stone workshop and quarry practice

**Short description.** Working stone from rough block to finished face.

Stone is unforgiving and the training reflects it: what is cut away does not come
back, and an apprentice spends a long time on rough work before being allowed
near anything that shows. The progression is from splitting and squaring, through
dressing and facing, to setting and building, with the master checking line,
bed and joint at every stage. Correction is mostly a matter of being shown the
fault and made to live with the consequence of it.

The same yard teaches the carved and modelled work. A mason's shop produces
mouldings, capitals, figures and lettering as well as plain walling, and the
apprentice who has learned to read and work a block is the person taught to
carve one. The two are one progression rather than two trades: understanding how
the stone will behave is what makes the expressive work possible, and the same
chisels and the same hands do both.

**What it teaches.** The preparation and assembly of stone or other masonry units
into stable construction, and the creation of expressive three-dimensional forms
in a learned material practice.

**Boundaries and context.** This is working the stone, not winning it. Extraction
at the face is a different practice, learned in a different place and taught in
its own right, and a mason is not by that fact a miner.

### C07 — Mine and quarry apprenticeship

**Short description.** Working an extraction face and getting in and out of it.

Extraction is learned underground or at the face, from the people already
working it, and the instruction is relentlessly practical: where to cut, how much
to take, what the ground is doing, when to stop and prop. The corrections that
matter are about the workings rather than the material — a face driven greedily
or a roof left unsupported endangers everyone, and a learner is watched closely
and told sharply until the caution is habitual.

Getting about is part of the trade. Faces, shafts, benches and spoil have to be
reached and left, often on poor footing and in poor light, and workers are taught
to ascend and descend properly with the supports and lines available rather than
scrambling. In a quarry it is the face and the wall; underground it is the shaft
and the ladder way. Either way it is trained, because the people who learn it
casually are the people who fall.

**What it teaches.** The planned extraction of useful material while managing the
workings and surrounding ground, and the trained ascent and descent of difficult
surfaces using secure movement and suitable supports.

**Boundaries and context.** Winning the material, not working it: dressing and
building the stone, and smelting and forging the ore, are separate trades. Living
away from settlement is not taught at the face, however remote the workings are.

### C13 — Lock and mechanism workshop

**Short description.** Making, fitting and repairing locks and small mechanisms.

A locksmith's shop is a small-metalwork shop with an exacting specialism, and an
apprentice is trained to a fineness the general trades do not require. The work
is making, fitting and above all repairing: most of what comes in is a lock that
has failed, and the apprentice learns by taking mechanisms apart, understanding
why this one stopped working, and being corrected when the diagnosis was wrong.
Fitting is the other discipline, since a lock that is perfect in the hand and
wrong in its door is useless.

The shop teaches the understanding behind the work as well as the work. A lock is
a mechanism, and the apprentice is taught to reason about mechanisms generally —
what moves what, what bears the load, where the wear will come, why this
arrangement will bind. And its parts are metal the shop works itself: an
apprentice works at the forge and the bench, shaping, joining and repairing
them, because a locksmith who cannot make a spring or a ward cannot make a lock.

**What it teaches.** The making, fitting and repair of locks and their working
parts; the practical analysis of forces, loads and motion in structures and
machines; and the controlled shaping, joining and repair of metal into useful
objects.

**Boundaries and context.** This is the maker's side of the trade. It is a
workshop practice, and it is not covert entry, which is learned somewhere else
entirely and by other people.

### C14 — Trap and device workshop

**Short description.** Building constructed traps and setting them where they
work.

A trap is built and then placed, and the workshop that teaches one teaches both,
because neither half is worth anything alone. The learner builds under
supervision — frame, spring, trigger, release — and is corrected on the mechanism
until it acts reliably at the intended provocation and not otherwise. A trap that
goes off when carried or fails when trodden on is the ordinary failure, and
learning to eliminate it is most of the making.

Then the learner is taken out and made to set them. Choosing the place, reading
the approach, concealing the device and tending it afterwards are a distinct
competence, and the correction is supplied by empty traps. A maker who cannot set
produces devices that sit in the woods untouched. The mechanical understanding
runs through both halves, and it runs backwards as well: someone who has built
and set traps to this standard can usually see how an unfamiliar one is meant to
work, and take it out of action.

**What it teaches.** The design, construction, maintenance and technical
disarming or bypass of constructed traps; the practical analysis of forces,
loads and motion in mechanisms; and the selection, placement and tending of
animal traps to take particular quarry.

**Boundaries and context.** Constructed devices and their placement. It is not a
hunting practice in the wider sense — reading a country, following quarry and
taking it by other means are learned elsewhere — and it is not a locksmith's
trade.

### C10 — Brewing and preserving practice

**Short description.** Brewing, cooking and keeping what the season gives.

Brewing and keeping food are one annual cycle, and in the household or the
brewhouse they are learned as one. What comes in has to be dealt with before it
spoils: some is eaten, some is cooked down, some is salted or dried, and some is
brewed. The learner works through a full round of seasons under someone who has
done many, and the correction arrives on its own schedule — a batch that soured,
a store that went over, a cask that did not finish — and is discussed when it
does.

The practice is therefore taught as a matter of stages and judgment rather than
recipes. Fermentation is managed rather than performed, and so is a preservation:
both are started, watched, adjusted and judged finished, and both are learned by
being made to say what is happening in the vessel before being told. Cooking is
not an adjacent skill here but the ground of the whole, and the specialist
keeping of food — choosing a method suited to the food and conditions, carrying
it through its stages, and judging whether the result is keeping — is taught
within the same practice.

**What it teaches.** The preparation and controlled fermentation of brewed
drinks; the preparation and transformation of ingredients into food and meals;
and the specialist preparation and storage of food so that it remains usable
beyond its ordinary keeping time.

**Boundaries and context.** A household or brewhouse practice on what the season
supplies. Growing it is a different practice, and wine is made in its own.

### C11 — Vineyard and winemaking practice

**Short description.** Growing the fruit and making the wine from it.

Wine is made vintage by vintage, and the practice is learned across repeated
ones. A learner joins a vineyard through its full round — pruning,
tending, watching the fruit, picking at the moment judged right, and then the
pressing, fermentation and long maturation — and does it again, and again. The
correction is annual and unanswerable: what is in the cask a year later is the
verdict on what was done, and the teaching consists largely of going back over a
vintage to establish why it is what it is.

The two halves are the same practice because the wine is decided in the vineyard.
How the vines were pruned, how the season ran and when the fruit was taken set
limits that the cellar cannot exceed, and a winemaker who does not grow does not
understand what he is given. Learners are accordingly trained in the cultivation
as seriously as in the cellar work, and are held to both.

**What it teaches.** The preparation, fermentation and maturation of wine within
a learned practice, and the establishment, tending and harvesting of cultivated
crops.

**Boundaries and context.** The vine and the wine. This is not a kitchen
practice: it teaches no cooking, and the specialist keeping of food belongs to
practices that teach the cooking it rests on.

### C15 — Alchemical workshop practice

**Short description.** Sustained alchemical operation with the apparatus it
needs.

An alchemical workshop is a working place of sustained heats, vessels and long
operations, and it is learned by tending them. The apprentice starts by keeping
the heat,
watching a vessel and recording what happens, which is the greater part of the
work, and is corrected on attention and record before ever being trusted with a
preparation. The instruction is within a particular tradition — its
understanding of materials, its sequences, its terms — and the learner is trained
in that tradition rather than in alchemy at large.

Two practical competences carry the whole of it. Separating and collecting a
substance by driving it off and catching it again is the apparatus the workshop
runs on, and it is taught as a craft: managing the heat, the vessel and the
receiver, and judging the fraction. And the products have to be prepared,
measured, kept and issued properly — an alchemical preparation that is
mismeasured or has degraded on the shelf is worse than none, so the learner is
held to the remedy practice's standard of preparation, measuring, preservation
and dispensing.

**What it teaches.** The learned investigation and transformation of materials
within a particular alchemical tradition; the controlled separation and
collection of substances through vaporization and condensation; and the
preparation, measuring, preservation and dispensing of medicines within a learned
remedy practice.

**Boundaries and context.** The workshop and its operations, within one
tradition. It teaches nothing of the treatment of patients: an alchemist prepares
and dispenses, and does not diagnose.

## Offices, courts and the street

### K11 — Court service practice

**Short description.** Serving in a court and knowing how it is actually
organised.

A court is learned from inside it, in a junior place, over years. The newcomer
carries messages, attends, keeps a record and is present at a great deal they do
not yet understand, and the teaching is done by the officers above them in short
and pointed corrections: that request goes to a different officer, that man's
opinion carries more weight than his office suggests, that matter is not
unresolved but waiting, you addressed the wrong person first. Much of it is
never set out systematically, and the learner is expected to assemble it by
attending carefully.

Most of court service is ordinary work done dependably. Business recurs,
obligations fall due, offices divide responsibility, and somebody has to keep all
of it moving whatever else is happening — which is why the administration is the
daily substance of the practice rather than an adjunct to it. Around that sit the
three things that make the work possible: the conventions of the society the
court belongs to, a working sense of where power actually lies as against where
it formally sits, and the arms and emblems by which everyone present announces
who they are. The practice also carries the specialist understanding of formal
precedence and presentation, and of how a court establishment's offices,
obligations and recurring business are properly organised.

**What it teaches.** The organization of recurring work, resources, records and
procedures so responsibilities are carried out dependably; the knowledge and
practical use of the social conventions of a society; the understanding of power,
institutions, factions and the processes through which collective decisions are
made; the recognition and interpretation of arms, emblems and associated claims;
and the specialist practices of courtly protocol and court administration.

**Boundaries and context.** One court, and the society it belongs to. The
conventions and the arms are that tradition's; another court's may differ
considerably. This teaches service in the establishment, not the exercise of the
authority it serves.

### K10 — Heraldic office practice

**Short description.** Arms, descent and the ceremony that uses them.

A heraldic officer is trained by another, and the training is documentary and
ceremonial at once. The learner blazons and identifies arms until it is quick and
accurate, is sent to establish who bore what and on what grounds, and has the
findings examined — because a heraldic opinion is a claim about somebody's
standing, and a careless one causes real trouble. The corrections are exacting
about evidence and about the difference between what is recorded, what is
asserted and what is merely usual.

The office's work cannot be done inside the armory alone. Arms carry descent, and
descent is a historical question: the learner is trained to study past people,
events and institutions properly, and to follow a claim through the record rather
than accept the version presented. And the arms exist to be used, on occasions
where precedence and presentation are the point, so the officer learns the
conventions of the society whose ceremonies they serve, together with the
specialist understanding of formal precedence and conduct within a court.

**What it teaches.** The recognition and interpretation of arms, emblems and
associated claims within a heraldic tradition; the knowledge and practical use of
a society's social conventions; the informed study and interpretation of past
people, events and institutions; and the specialist practice of courtly protocol.

**Boundaries and context.** A heraldic tradition, and one society's conventions.
The office establishes and interprets; the administration of a court
establishment is a separate practice.

### K22 — Contemplative discipline practice

**Short description.** Trained stillness, trained memory and the reading of
people it sharpens.

Contemplative houses teach a discipline, and they teach it as a discipline —
daily, for years, under supervision, with an elder who asks what actually
happened during the exercise and corrects what is described. The learner is
trained to enter and hold a particular state of mind and to sustain it through
tedium, discomfort and distraction. It is slow and largely uneventful training,
and the correction is conversational rather than demonstrative, because there is
nothing to watch.

A corpus is memorised alongside it. Such traditions carry a body of material that
must be held accurately and recalled in order, and they teach deliberate method
for holding it rather than leaving it to raw memory. The third element is
trained just as deliberately and alongside the other two: in a community where
people live at close quarters and speak carefully, reading what people actually
mean is drawn out and corrected by elders rather than left to instinct.

**What it teaches.** A trained discipline for entering and maintaining a mental
state suited to ceremonies, rituals and other demanding practices; the deliberate
use of memory techniques to organize information for later recall; and the
interpretation of people's motives, concerns and likely intentions from their
words, conduct and circumstances.

**Boundaries and context.** The discipline, the memory and the attentiveness. It
is not doctrinal study and not religious service before a congregation; a
contemplative may be neither a scholar nor a priest.

### K24 — Watch and guard practice

**Short description.** Standing a watch, knowing the ground, and handling people
without a fight.

Guard duty is genuinely taught, and the teaching is done by the watch itself.
A new man walks with an experienced one and is instructed continuously: look
there, not there; that door was shut last night; that is the third time he has
passed. Standing a watch attentively for hours is a trained competence and a
tiring one, and it is corrected — the things missed are pointed out afterwards,
and missing them twice is noticed.

The other half is the ground and the people on it. A watchman learns whose
district this is, who belongs in it, what the ordinary traffic of each hour looks
like and who to ask, because a guard without that knowledge sees nothing
significant. And the work turns on handling people without violence: drunks,
disputes, small crowds and men who want to be talked past. Presenting oneself so
that people stand down is taught explicitly and practised constantly, since the
watch's whole advantage is settling things before they become fights.

**What it teaches.** The attentive noticing and discrimination of relevant
detail, including during a deliberate search; practical knowledge of the informal
contacts, opportunities and conventions of an urban milieu or settlement; and the
use of credible threat and forceful presentation to pressure another person's
decision.

**Boundaries and context.** The watch, in a place it knows. This is not a weapon
course and not drill: what is taught is standing a watch and controlling a
situation, not fighting. Investigation of a crime afterwards is a different
practice.

### K25 — Street theft practice

**Short description.** Taking what is worth taking, from places it was not meant
to be taken from.

This is learned from people who do it, usually young and usually in company, and
the correction is entirely practical. Hands are trained first: small objects
palmed, passed and exchanged until the movement disappears, practised against
each other and then in the street, with the failures discussed. Nobody teaches it
as a course, but it is unmistakably taught — by demonstration, by repetition, and
by the sharp instruction of someone who has been caught and does not intend to be
again.

Two other things are learned in the same company. Knowing what is worth taking is
half the trade, and it is taught: what that is, what it will fetch, what is
traceable and what is not worth the risk, judged at a glance and corrected
afterwards by what it actually fetched. And reaching things is the other half —
walls, roofs, sills and stairs, ascended and descended using whatever supports
are there. The particular specialist practice of lifting from what
somebody is carrying, with its own demands of access, timing and unobtrusive
handling, is taught within this same company.

**What it teaches.** The controlled manipulation of small objects so the movement
or exchange is difficult to notice; the assessment of goods by their quality,
condition, workmanship and likely value; the trained ascent and descent of
difficult surfaces; and the specialist concealed manipulation of another person's
carried belongings.

**Boundaries and context.** Street theft, learned in the street. It is not
covert entry against secured premises, which is a separate practice, and it is
not a violent trade.

### K21 — Applied calculation practice

**Short description.** Calculation put to work on accounts, measures and ground.

Reckoning is taught here as something done rather than studied. The learner works
methods until they are reliable — and then, crucially, is made to apply them to
real material and is corrected when the answer is right and useless or wrong in a
way the method should have caught. Checking is drilled as hard as calculating: a
practitioner is trained to know whether a figure can be believed, which is what
distinguishes applied calculation from arithmetic.

Two applications supply the correction. Accounts must be recorded in order and
made to balance, and a discrepancy is an unambiguous verdict on the work — the
learner keeps real books and reconciles them until they reconcile. Ground must be
measured, and the measurement checked and closed, which gives the same
discipline a physical form: positions, dimensions, levels and boundaries
established systematically, where an error shows up as a figure that will not
close. The three are taught together because each teaches what the others cannot.

**What it teaches.** The use of learned methods to reason about quantities,
relationships, shapes and patterns; the orderly recording and reconciliation of
quantities, transactions and outstanding obligations; and the systematic
measurement of positions, dimensions, levels and boundaries on the ground.

**Boundaries and context.** Calculation applied to accounts, measures and ground.
It is not the wider learned study of the world, and it is not the conduct of a
trading or banking house: it teaches the reckoning such a house depends on.

### K18 — Underworld association practice

**Short description.** Moving inside an illicit milieu without being handed over.

What is taught here is how to exist inside an illicit world, and the teachers are
the people already in it. Instruction is by admission and warning: this is who
is owed deference, this is whose protection is real, this is what is settled among
us and what is not, this is what happened to the last man who talked. The
correction is delivered as consequence, and the practice being formed is mostly
judgment about people — whom to deal with, on what terms, and how far.

Around that sits the ordinary knowledge of the settlement the milieu lives
inside, since an illicit network is not separate from the place it operates in:
who trades in what, which officials are approachable, where a thing can be moved
or a person found. And there is a constant practical discipline of not having
things found: choosing and arranging a hiding place that survives an ordinary
search is learned early, corrected by searches, and never stops being useful.

**What it teaches.** Practical knowledge of the networks, conventions and
dealings of a particular illicit milieu; practical knowledge of the informal
contacts, opportunities and conventions of an urban milieu or settlement; and the
selection and arrangement of a hiding place that keeps an object from ordinary
discovery.

**Boundaries and context.** One milieu, in one place: its conventions transfer
imperfectly, if at all, to another city's. This is association and knowledge,
not a trade in itself — it teaches no theft, no entry and no violence.

### K17 — Gaming-house practice

**Short description.** The games, the odds, the players and the hands.

A gaming house teaches its own, and thoroughly. The learner is put to work in it,
watches a great deal of play, and is taken through the games properly: the rules,
the run of them, and above all what a stake is actually worth in a given position
— which is corrected by losses and by the patient explanation of people who have
stopped making that mistake. Play itself is the exercise, repeated until judgment
under stake becomes steady rather than hopeful.

The other half of the house is the people. Reading a player — what they are
holding, what they can afford, whether they mean it — is where the advantage
lies, and it is taught explicitly, discussed between rounds and corrected
against what the play turns out to have been. And hands are trained: a gaming
house's trade involves fine control of small objects, and whether that control
is used honestly or otherwise, it is learned in the same room from the same
people.

**What it teaches.** The practiced understanding and play of games in which
participants stake something on an uncertain result; the interpretation of
people's motives, concerns and likely intentions from their words, conduct and
circumstances; and the controlled manipulation of small objects so the movement
or exchange is difficult to notice.

**Boundaries and context.** The house and its games. It is not a wider criminal
association, and the fine handwork learned here is the table's, not a
pickpocket's trade.

### K16 — Documentary hand practice

**Short description.** Reproducing a documentary hand convincingly enough to
pass.

This is a study practice with a dishonest purpose, and it is the study that makes
it work. The learner is taught to read difficult, unfamiliar and old writing
properly first — hands, abbreviations, formulas, the conventions of particular
offices and periods — and is corrected on readings until they are defensible. Only
someone who can genuinely read a documentary tradition can reproduce one, and the
apprenticeship runs in that order.

The reproduction is then trained against the real thing. The learner copies,
compares, is shown what gave the attempt away and does it again: the wrong
formula, the hand that belongs to the wrong decade, the material that is right
for the wrong office. Judging the material is the third competence and it is
inseparable from the other two, since the practitioner must assess the
materials, marks and workmanship of the documents in question as closely as an
examiner would, and know what the genuine article is worth to whoever wants the
copy.

**What it teaches.** The creation or alteration of documents and credentials
intended to pass as genuine examples of a particular documentary practice; the
analysis of difficult, unfamiliar or historical writing to recover a defensible
reading; and the assessment of goods by their quality, condition, workmanship
and likely value.

**Boundaries and context.** Documents. It teaches no impersonation of a person
and no entry anywhere; what it produces is a piece of writing that stands up to
inspection.

## Study, sky and religion

### K01 — Scholarly study practice

**Short description.** Finding, holding and checking what is already known.

Scholarly study is an apprenticeship like any other, conducted over whatever
sources the tradition works from. The
learner is set a question and sent to answer it, and the answer is taken apart:
where did this come from, what does it actually say, what does it rest on, why do
you believe it rather than the other account. The corrections are almost entirely
about method, and they continue until the learner stops assembling opinions and
starts assembling evidence that can be defended by someone else later.

Holding what is found is the second half, and traditions that take study
seriously teach it deliberately rather than relying on a good memory. The learner
is given method — ordered, deliberate technique for retaining material and
recalling it in usable form — and is corrected on it, because a scholar who
cannot bring the relevant passage to mind at the moment of argument has not
finished the work. The two together are what the practice is: finding out, and
keeping hold of it.

**What it teaches.** The deliberate gathering, comparison and evaluation of
evidence to answer a question, and the deliberate use of memory techniques to
organize information for later recall.

**Boundaries and context.** Study in general, of whatever the tradition studies.
It supplies no subject of its own: it teaches how to find out and how to hold,
not what is true about the sky, the past or the world. Reckoning is a separate
competence, taught in its own right where a scholarly route needs it.

### K02 — Natural philosophy practice

**Short description.** Arguing about what the world is, against what can be
observed of it.

Natural philosophy is taught by argument, in company. The learner is required to
state a position about the nature of things, defend it against people who
disagree, and be shown where the reasoning fails — not where the conclusion is
unpopular. What is trained is the handling of arguments themselves: what follows
from what, which objection is fatal and which merely awkward, and where a
confident-sounding position is resting on nothing.

The argument is held against evidence, which is what distinguishes this from
philosophy pursued alone. The learner is made to gather what bears on a question
and weigh it honestly, and is corrected for reaching past it. And the sky is the
standing test case: it is the part of the world that can be observed
systematically and whose regularities can be checked, and a tradition of this
kind teaches its observation as a matter of course, because it is where claims
about the order of things meet something that will contradict them.

**What it teaches.** The systematic examination of arguments about knowledge,
conduct and fundamental ideas; the deliberate gathering, comparison and
evaluation of evidence to answer a question; and the systematic observation and
understanding of celestial bodies and their recurring motions.

**Boundaries and context.** This is inquiry into what the world is. It does not
teach the reading of the sky for people's affairs, which belongs to a different
tradition with different methods, and it is not a religious study.

### K03 — Celestial observation practice

**Short description.** Watching the sky methodically and calculating from it.

Astronomical practice is learned by keeping a record over a long time. The
learner observes on a schedule, in bad conditions as well as good, and writes
down what was seen with enough care that it will still be usable years later.
Correction comes from the record itself: a careless observation shows up as a
result that does not fit the ones around it, and the teacher's work is largely
making the learner see why their own entries cannot all be right.

Observation without calculation is only a diary, and the two are taught as one
practice. The learner is worked through the methods needed to derive something
from what was recorded — positions, intervals, recurrences — and is held to them
until the reckoning is dependable, since an error in the arithmetic is
indistinguishable from an error in the sky. The third competence is taught
alongside the other two, in the same nightly attention: reading the weather
decides whether there will be an observation at all, so a working observatory
instructs its learners in it and corrects them on it.

**What it teaches.** The systematic observation and understanding of celestial
bodies and their recurring motions; the use of learned methods to reason about
quantities, relationships, shapes and patterns; and the interpretation of
environmental signs and recurring conditions to judge likely weather.

**Boundaries and context.** Observation and calculation. Interpreting the sky for
people and their affairs is a separate tradition, learned separately, however
much it draws on the same observations.

### K04 — Astrological reading practice

**Short description.** Reading the sky for people, in a tradition that says how.

An astrologer is trained within a tradition, and the tradition supplies both the
method and the standard. The learner is taught what the tradition holds
significant and how it holds it, works readings under supervision, and has them
corrected against the tradition's own rules — this configuration is not read that
way, this factor outweighs that one, you have gone past what the method
authorises. What is being formed is a practitioner who can produce a reading
another practitioner of the same tradition would recognise as properly done.

Doing it requires knowing where the sky actually is, so the observational
competence is taught alongside and taken seriously: positions and motions must be
established correctly before anything is read from them. And the reading is
delivered to a person, in a society, about matters that are frequently delicate.
Traditions accordingly teach the conventions of the society they practise in —
what may be asked, what may be said, on what occasions and to whom — and carry
the specialist interpretation of recognised signs and occurrences as omens within
the method.

**What it teaches.** The interpretation of celestial patterns according to a
learned astrological tradition; the systematic observation and understanding of
celestial bodies and their recurring motions; the knowledge and practical use of
a society's social conventions; and the specialist reading of omens within the
tradition.

**Boundaries and context.** One tradition, with its own significances and rules;
another's readings need not agree. This is not natural philosophy, and it is not
a religious office.

### K05 — Divinatory practice

**Short description.** A maintained and reproducibly taught method of
divination.

What makes this a taught practice rather than an individual gift is that the
method is fixed and can be handed on. The learner is trained in a defined
procedure — how it is prepared, performed, read and concluded — and performs it
repeatedly under an established practitioner who corrects the performance and the
reading against the tradition's own standards. Two practitioners of the same
tradition, given the same case, should recognise each other's work, and the
training exists to make that so.

The method does not stand on its own. A divinatory practice belongs to a
religious tradition and draws its meanings from it, so the learner is taught the
tradition's teachings, practices and significances as part of learning the method
— what the signs mean here rests on what is believed here. Within that, the
specialist interpretation of recognised signs and occurrences as omens is taught
as a distinct refinement: which features the tradition treats as significant, and
where it supplies no clear meaning at all.

**What it teaches.** The performance and interpretation of a defined divinatory
method within its tradition; the knowledge and understanding of the teachings,
practices and meanings of a religious tradition; and the specialist reading of
omens within the method.

**Boundaries and context.** One method and one tradition. It is not astrological
practice, which reads the sky by its own rules, and it is not religious service
before a congregation.

### K06 — Religious service practice

**Short description.** Serving a religion in front of the people who practise
it.

A religious practitioner is formed by serving, under supervision, in front of a
congregation. The learner assists, then conducts parts of the observance, then
conducts it, and is corrected afterwards on what was done and how it was
received. The teaching is doctrinal and practical at once, because the two are
inseparable in front of people: an error of understanding and an error of manner
both damage the service, and both are pointed out.

Serving means speaking. A practitioner explains, argues, exhorts and consoles
before an assembly, and is trained to prepare and deliver speech that does it —
corrected on structure, clarity and effect, not only on content. It also means
knowing the people: a religion is practised inside a society, and the
practitioner is expected to understand its conventions, occasions and
expectations well enough to serve within them. The tradition's systematic study
of its own doctrines and interpretive arguments is taught within the same
formation, as the learned side of the same service.

**What it teaches.** The knowledge and understanding of the teachings, practices
and meanings of a religious tradition; the knowledge and practical use of a
society's social conventions; the preparation and delivery of speech that
explains, argues or persuades before an audience; and the specialist systematic
study of the tradition's doctrines and interpretive arguments.

**Boundaries and context.** One tradition, served among its own people. It is not
a divinatory method, and it is not a contemplative discipline: a priest need be
neither a diviner nor a contemplative.

### K07 — Instruction and schooling practice

**Short description.** Teaching deliberately, and being corrected on the
teaching.

Teaching well is a competence, and where it is taken seriously it is taught.
A learner takes lessons under an experienced teacher's observation and is
corrected on the teaching rather than the subject: the explanation that assumed
what the class did not know, the question asked in a way nobody could answer, the
error left standing because correcting it would have been uncomfortable, the
pace that lost the back of the room. Doing it and being watched doing it is the
whole method.

Two things support it and are trained alongside. A teacher must hold a great deal
in order and produce the right part of it at the right moment, so deliberate
method for retaining and recalling material is taught rather than left to native
memory — and it is taught doubly, since the pupils need the same method and the
teacher is expected to pass it on. And a teacher speaks to an assembly:
preparing and delivering speech that explains and holds attention is the
practical form the work takes, and it is corrected as such.

**What it teaches.** The practice of helping another person learn through
explanation, demonstration, guided practice and useful correction; the deliberate
use of memory techniques to organize information for later recall; and the
preparation and delivery of speech that explains, argues or persuades before an
audience.

**Boundaries and context.** The practice of instruction itself. It supplies no
subject: what a teacher trained here knows to teach comes from wherever they
learned it. Teaching a craft at the bench, or arms in a school, are separate
practices with their own demands.

### K08 — Interpreting and language mediation practice

**Short description.** Standing between two parties who do not share a language.

Interpreting is a practice distinct from knowing languages, and it is trained
distinctly. The learner works alongside an established interpreter in real
exchanges and is corrected afterwards on the mediation: the sense that was
carried but the force was not, the hedge that was flattened into a refusal, the
aside that should not have been passed on, the turn taken too soon. What is being
built is fidelity under pressure — rendering what was meant, at speed, without
becoming a third party to the conversation.

Meaning does not survive being moved between languages without also being moved
between the assumptions around them, and the interpreter is trained in the
conventions of the societies they stand between: what a form of address implies
here and there, what politeness or bluntness signifies, what an occasion demands.
Reading the people in front of them is the other trained competence, and it is
constant — an interpreter must judge what each party actually wants, what they
have not said, and when a misunderstanding is forming, because they will see it
before either of them does.

**What it teaches.** The accurate rendering of meaning between languages in
speech or writing; the knowledge and practical use of the social conventions of a
society; and the interpretation of people's motives, concerns and likely
intentions from their words, conduct and circumstances.

**Boundaries and context.** This teaches the practice of mediating, never a
language. Which languages an interpreter commands is entirely separate from this
training, and the practice assumes rather than supplies them.

### K09 — Chorographic and geographic study

**Short description.** Assembling what is known about places and setting it down.

This is a study of places conducted mostly out of other people's reports. The
learner is set to assemble what is known about a region — from accounts,
itineraries, older descriptions and the testimony of travellers — and is
corrected on the assembly: this account is derived from that one and is not
independent confirmation, this distance is copied from a source that guessed it,
these two names are the same place. The discipline is source discipline, and it
is the greater part of the training.

The result is set down as a map or a description, and making it is taught as a
craft with its own standards. The learner drafts, has the draft criticised and
revises: what is shown as certain that is not, what has been distorted to fit the
sheet, what a reader will misunderstand. The three competences are one practice
because each constrains the others — what can be assembled honestly determines
what can be drawn honestly, and attempting to draw a region is what reveals how
little is actually known about it.

**What it teaches.** The knowledge of places and regions and the relationships
among their physical and human features; the construction and revision of maps
and charts that communicate spatial relationships clearly; and the deliberate
gathering, comparison and evaluation of evidence to answer a question.

**Boundaries and context.** A study practice, pursued largely at a desk from
reports. It is not ground survey, and it is not navigation: a chorographer
describes and maps the world without necessarily being able to measure a field or
find a way across one.

## Groups whose practice has grown

The two Groups below already have accepted prose, but the canonical V2 catalogue
records a wider taught practice than the accepted pages describe. The prose here
is proposed to replace the accepted pages for these two Groups only.

### F04 — Running and athletic field practice

**Short description.** Conditioned movement over distance, height and water.

Physical training of this kind is deliberate, supervised and repetitive. A
learner trains under someone who trains people — at a ground, a school or a
household that keeps a trainer — and the work is set, watched and adjusted: this
distance at this pace, this movement until the form holds, more of that and less
of this. The correction is about technique and about conditioning together, since
the point is not a single performance but a body that can be relied on to perform
repeatedly.

The training is not confined to the track. A programme of this kind sets
learners to move over whatever is in the way, and the ground supplies height and
water as readily as distance. Getting up and down difficult surfaces securely,
and moving and supporting oneself in water, are trained within the same
programme — but each has its own techniques and its own practice environment,
and each is corrected in its own right. Conditioning carries across them;
technique does not.

**What it teaches.** Trained performance in running, including speed, pacing,
efficient movement and sustained effort over terrain; trained body control for
jumping, vaulting, balancing and related athletic movement; trained movement and
support of the body in water; and the trained ascent and descent of difficult
surfaces using secure movement and suitable supports.

**Boundaries and context.** Conditioning and movement, with each technique
learned in its own right. Dance requires a movement tradition and expressive or
formal performance instruction, and is not taught here. It is not a fighting
practice of any kind — neither unarmed fighting nor trained evasion is supplied
by general athletic movement — and it teaches no craft: what it produces is a
body trained to cover ground.

### F09 — Rigged-vessel deck apprenticeship

**Short description.** Deck and rigging work on a rigged vessel.

A deck apprentice learns aboard, from the hands. The work is handling the vessel
and running its routines — watches, sail handling, ground tackle, the ordinary
labour of keeping a ship working — and the correction is immediate, public and
unsentimental, because on a rigged vessel a slow or wrong hand is a danger to
other people. Competence is established by being trusted with progressively more
of the work.

A rigged vessel runs on rope and is worked from aloft, and both are trained
accordingly. Every line has a purpose, a place and a knot proper to it, and the
apprentice is drilled until the right one is made correctly in the dark — and
drilled as thoroughly on inspecting a working line, diagnosing why one has
failed, and splicing it. Going aloft is likewise taught rather than assumed:
climbing the rigging and working there safely is learned under instruction
rather than picked up. Vessels and rigging traditions differ, and what an
apprentice is drilled on is the practice of the ship they learn in. And the
ship's people read the weather constantly,
because sail is set and shortened on that judgment; an apprentice is brought into
it and corrected on it long before ever being asked to make the decision.

**What it teaches.** The practical handling of a vessel and the working routines
needed to operate it on water; the skilled tying, joining, securing and practical
use of ropes and lines; the trained ascent and descent of difficult surfaces
using secure movement and suitable supports; and the interpretation of
environmental signs and recurring conditions to judge likely weather.

**Boundaries and context.** The deck and the rigging of a rigged vessel. It is
not navigation — finding the way is a separate practice with separate
instruction — and it is not shipbuilding. Making cordage is a manufacturing
trade rather than the working of finished rope, and the design and construction
of sails is another practice again.
